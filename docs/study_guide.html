How to use this guide

The guide is ordered so that each section only uses ideas explained before it. If you are completely new, read it top to bottom once. If you already know some remote sensing, jump to the pipeline and use the glossary when a term is unfamiliar.

  • Concepts first (sections 3–8): satellites, radar, maps and coordinates, the datasets used, image analysis and accuracy measures.
  • Then the project (sections 9–13): each processing step, the code files that do it, the results and the decisions behind them.
  • Check yourself: most concept sections end with short questions. Open them to see the answer.

The full write-up is the 16-page report at docs/report/Nagaon_Flood_Project_Report.pdf. This guide explains the ideas behind it in more detail and maps them to the code.

The project in brief

Assam, in north-east India, floods almost every monsoon. In June 2022, heavy rain and high water in the Brahmaputra and its tributaries flooded large parts of Nagaon district. During a monsoon, clouds hide the ground from ordinary camera-like satellites, so the project uses radar, which sees through cloud.

The project answers three research questions from the proposal:

  1. How did the flooded area change between the June satellite passes?
  2. Where did flooding reach, or come close to, built-up land (houses, roads)?
  3. How much do the answers change if the detection thresholds change?
The method in one sentence

For each June radar image, mark a pixel as flooded if it became much darker than in the dry season and is now dark enough to be open water, then remove places that cannot realistically flood, measure the areas, and compare with other flood maps.

Remote sensing basics

Remote sensing means measuring the Earth's surface from a distance, usually from satellites or aircraft. A satellite sensor records how much energy comes back from each small patch of ground. Each patch becomes one pixel of an image.

Passive and active sensors

  • Passive (optical) sensors such as Sentinel-2 or Landsat record reflected sunlight, much like a camera. They give natural-looking images but cannot see through cloud and need daylight.
  • Active sensors send out their own signal and record the echo. Radar sends microwaves, which pass through cloud and work at night. This is why the project uses radar: in a monsoon flood the sky is often fully covered.

Resolution, in four kinds

  • Spatial: the ground size of a pixel. Sentinel-1 products here use 10 m pixels, so one pixel is 100 m² and 10,000 pixels make one km².
  • Temporal: how often the same place is imaged (the revisit time). Here, 12 days per orbit track.
  • Spectral (optical) or polarisation (radar): which kinds of signal are recorded. Sentinel-1 records two polarisations, VV and VH.
  • Radiometric: how finely brightness is measured.

Orbits, passes and tracks

Sentinel-1 circles the Earth from pole to pole. On the ascending pass it travels south to north; over India this happens around 18:00 local time. On the descending pass it travels north to south, around 05:00–06:00. Each repeating orbit path has a number called the relative orbit or track. Images from the same track always look at the ground from the same angle, so they can be compared directly. Images from different tracks look from different angles and should not be compared pixel by pixel.

Two satellites, Sentinel-1A and 1B, used to share the work, giving a 6-day revisit. Sentinel-1B failed in December 2021, so in 2022 each track was imaged only every 12 days. The project uses two tracks (77 descending and 41 ascending) to get six June images instead of three.

Why can't the project just use a normal satellite photo of the flood?

Because during the monsoon clouds cover the ground most of the time. Optical sensors record reflected sunlight, which cannot pass through cloud. Radar provides its own microwave signal, which passes through cloud.

A 10 m pixel covers how much area, and how many pixels make 1 km²?

10 m × 10 m = 100 m². One km² is 1,000,000 m², so 10,000 pixels. The code uses PIX_KM2 = 1e-4 (one pixel is 0.0001 km²).

How radar sees floods

The project's radar is a synthetic aperture radar (SAR). It looks sideways from the satellite, sends short microwave pulses, and records how much energy bounces back. That returned strength is called backscatter. Each pixel's backscatter is stored as σ⁰ (sigma nought), the backscatter per unit area of ground.

Why flood water looks dark

What the radar sees depends mostly on surface roughness compared with the wavelength (about 5.6 cm for Sentinel-1's C-band).

  • Calm water acts like a mirror (specular reflection). The pulse bounces away from the satellite, so almost nothing comes back and water appears dark.
  • Rough land scatters energy in many directions (diffuse scattering), some of it back to the satellite, so it appears grey.
  • Buildings form a corner with the ground. The pulse bounces off the ground and then the wall, straight back to the satellite (double bounce). Buildings stay bright even when surrounded by water. This is why the project cannot see water among houses and measures exposure by distance instead (see step 7).

Two other effects matter. Wind roughens water, making it brighter and harder to detect. Trees scatter C-band energy within their canopy (volume scattering), so water under trees is mostly hidden. Longer wavelengths such as L-band (about 24 cm, used by ALOS-2) penetrate canopy better, which matters when comparing with the ALOS-2 reference map.

Decibels: the dB scale

Backscatter values span a huge range, so they are usually shown on a logarithmic scale in decibels: dB = 10 × log10(σ⁰). Every −10 dB means ten times less energy, and −3 dB means roughly half.

σ⁰ (linear)dBTypical meaning
0.1−10bright land, VV
0.03−15grey land, VH
0.01−20around the water/land boundary in VH
0.001−30very dark; near the sensor's noise level

A useful consequence: subtracting two dB values is the same as dividing the original values. A drop of 3 dB between the dry and flood images means the returned energy fell to about half. The project's change rule is written as a dB difference for this reason.

Polarisation: VV and VH

Radar waves vibrate in a particular orientation. VV means sent vertical, received vertical. VH means sent vertical, received horizontal (a "cross-polarised" channel). VH values are lower overall and respond strongly to vegetation, which makes the contrast between open water and land large. The project uses VH for detection and tests VV in the sensitivity analysis (VV maps about 10% less flooding).

Speckle

Radar images have a grainy salt-and-pepper texture called speckle. It comes from many small scatterers inside one pixel whose echoes add up randomly. It is not real ground detail, so it is reduced with a speckle filter. The project uses the Lee filter, which smooths flat areas strongly but keeps edges by looking at the local mean and variance in a 7 × 7 pixel window.

From raw data to usable images

The project uses Sentinel-1 GRD (Ground Range Detected) products in Google Earth Engine. Earth Engine has already applied the standard corrections: calibration to σ⁰, thermal noise removal, and terrain correction (placing each pixel at its true map position using an elevation model). The project then masks noisy image borders and applies the Lee filter.

A pixel goes from −16 dB in April to −24 dB in June. What happened to the returned energy, and is it likely flooded?

The change is −8 dB, so the returned energy fell to about one-sixth (10^(−0.8) ≈ 0.16). The June value of −24 dB is also below the water threshold of about −20 dB. Both conditions of the project's rule are met, so it would be marked as flood.

Why do flooded villages tend to be missed?

Buildings stay bright because of double bounce, and village houses in Assam are often under trees, whose canopy blocks C-band radar. Neither shows the darkening that the method looks for.

GIS basics

A geographic information system (GIS) stores, analyses and maps data that has a location. Two data models are used throughout the project.

Raster

A grid of pixels, each holding a value: radar brightness, elevation, or a class code such as 1 = flood. Stored here as GeoTIFF (.tif) files, which carry the grid's position on Earth.

Examples: flood_20220618_t77.tif, static.tif

Vector

Points, lines and polygons with attributes, such as a district outline or flood polygons. Stored here as GeoJSON, GeoPackage (.gpkg) and shapefile (.shp plus sidecar files).

Examples: nagaon_2022.geojson, reference_nagaon.gpkg

Coordinate reference systems

A coordinate reference system (CRS) defines what the numbers in a file mean on the Earth. Each CRS has an EPSG code.

  • EPSG:4326 (WGS 84) uses longitude and latitude in degrees. Good for storing locations, bad for measuring area, because a degree of longitude shrinks towards the poles.
  • EPSG:32646 (UTM zone 46N) is a projected system in metres, covering 90–96°E. The project puts every raster on one 10 m UTM 46N grid so that a pixel is always exactly 100 m² and areas are simple counts.

The district area is also checked as a geodesic area, measured on the curved Earth (2,577.6 km²). The grid count gives 2,575.6 km², a small difference from pixel edges at the boundary.

Common GIS operations used

  • Clip: cut a layer to the district outline (reference flood maps were clipped to Nagaon).
  • Rasterise: turn polygons into pixels on the grid, so vector and raster layers can be compared pixel by pixel.
  • Reproject: convert a layer from one CRS to another.
  • Dissolve: merge touching polygons into one shape.
  • Zonal statistics: summarise a raster within polygons, such as flooded km² per sub-district.
  • Nodata: a special pixel value meaning "no information". The flood maps use 255 for "not observed"; the downloaded stacks use −32768.

Administrative boundaries

Boundaries change over time, so the boundary must match the date studied. Hojai was split from Nagaon in 2016 and merged back on 31 December 2022, so in June 2022 Nagaon did not include Hojai. The project uses the district without Hojai (2,577.6 km²) and its seven 2011-census sub-districts as reporting units: Kaliabor, Samaguri, Rupahi, Dhing, Nagaon, Raha and Kampur.

Why not measure areas directly in latitude/longitude?

Degrees are not a fixed distance on the ground; a degree of longitude covers fewer metres further from the equator. A metric projection such as UTM gives pixels of a fixed size, so area is simply the pixel count times 100 m².

Elevation, slope and HAND

A DEM (digital elevation model) is a raster of ground height. Slope is how steeply it changes. Water collects on flat, low ground, so steep slopes are excluded. The project first tried slope from the SRTM DEM but rejected it: SRTM measures the top of trees and buildings, making the flat floodplain look bumpy. More than 10% of the lowest land showed slopes over 5°. The MERIT DEM has that vegetation height removed, so its slope works.

HAND (height above nearest drainage) gives, for every pixel, how far it sits above the nearest stream along the path water would flow. A field 1 m above a river can flood; a ridge 40 m above it cannot. Following Twele et al. (2016), pixels with HAND of 15 m or more are excluded. In Nagaon 95% of the district is below that, so this mask removes very little.

JRC water history: occurrence and seasonality

The JRC Global Surface Water dataset records water seen by Landsat from 1984 to 2021. Two of its bands are used, and the difference between them matters:

  • Occurrence: the percentage of all observations, over 1984–2021, in which the pixel was water.
  • Seasonality: the number of months the pixel had water in 2021 only, the last year of the record.

The project calls a pixel permanent water if its 2021 seasonality is 10 months or more, and keeps it out of the flood class. A pixel that was wet in older years but not in 2021 is not excluded. This was one of the corrections made after the external review.

WorldCover land cover

ESA WorldCover 2021 labels each 10 m pixel with a class: 10 tree cover, 20 shrubland, 30 grassland, 40 cropland, 50 built-up, 60 bare, 80 permanent water, 90 herbaceous wetland. In Nagaon, cropland covers 51%, tree cover 39% (including village homestead gardens) and built-up 2.5% (63.9 km²). The built-up class includes roads as well as buildings.

A pixel has JRC occurrence 30% but seasonality 0. Is it "permanent water" in this project?

No. Seasonality 0 means no water in 2021, so it is not excluded, even though it was wet in some earlier years. On 18 June, 161.8 km² of mapped flood lies on land like this.

Image analysis techniques

Change detection

Change detection compares an image from during the event with a baseline (reference) image of normal conditions. Here the baseline is the median of all January to mid-April 2022 images from the same track: 8 for track 77 and 8 for track 41. The median of several images is less affected by one odd image than a single dry-season picture. May was avoided because Nagaon was already flooding in mid-May 2022.

Change detection is stronger than looking at one image alone. A permanently dark surface (a smooth road, sand, a lake) is dark in both images and does not change, so it is not marked as new flooding.

Thresholding and histograms

A threshold is a cut-off value: pixels below it are called water, above it land. A histogram counts how many pixels have each value. In a scene with water and land it often has two peaks (it is bimodal), and a good threshold lies in the valley between them.

Otsu's method

Otsu's method picks a threshold automatically. It tries every possible cut-off and keeps the one that makes the two groups most different from each other (it maximises the between-class variance). It only works well if both groups are well represented in the histogram. Across a whole district, land pixels vastly outnumber water and the water peak can vanish.

The project therefore uses an edge-based version (Donchyts et al. 2016). It first finds land/water boundaries with the Canny edge detector, then takes only pixels within about 60 m of those edges, where water and land appear in similar numbers. Otsu is applied to that sample. If no edges are found, or the result is outside a sensible range for water (−26 to −15 dB in VH), a fixed fallback of −20 dB is used.

The classification rule

flood = (VHJune − VHdry < −3 dB) AND (VHJune < Otsu threshold)

then remove pixels where HAND ≥ 15 m, slope ≥ 5°, or 2021 seasonality ≥ 10 months, and remove flood patches smaller than 8 pixels.

Connected components and the minimum mapping unit

Single isolated "flood" pixels are usually noise. Connected-component labelling groups touching flood pixels into patches (here using 8-connectivity, so diagonal neighbours count). Patches smaller than 8 pixels (800 m²) are removed. This smallest size kept is the minimum mapping unit (MMU).

Morphology and distance transforms

  • Dilation grows a mask outward by a few pixels. It is used to widen detected edges into a sampling zone for Otsu.
  • A distance transform gives every pixel its straight-line (Euclidean) distance to the nearest flood pixel. Built-up pixels with a distance of 50 m or less count as exposed.

Exposure analysis

Exposure describes what was in harm's way. A direct overlap of flood pixels and built-up pixels is almost empty (0.14 km² over all dates), because double bounce keeps buildings bright. So the project measures built-up land within a distance of mapped water: 50 m as the main measure, with 30 m and 100 m as alternatives. This is proximity to flooding, not proof that buildings were damaged or people affected.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis asks how much the results change when the settings change. The project uses two designs:

  • One at a time: change one setting (say, the drop threshold from −1 to −6 dB) while keeping the others at their defaults.
  • Joint grid: change two settings together across a grid of 7 × 7 combinations, as in the threshold explorer below.
Why look only near edges before applying Otsu?

Otsu needs both classes in the histogram. Across the whole district, land is far more common than water, so the water peak can be too small to find. Near land/water edges, both classes appear in similar numbers.

Why does the rule need both a change condition and an absolute condition?

The change condition alone would mark land that darkened only slightly but is still clearly land, such as a field after rain. The absolute condition alone would mark surfaces that are always dark, such as permanent lakes or smooth bare ground. Requiring both keeps only pixels that became water.

Two cautions used in this project

A reference map is not the truth. Rapid-mapping products are made quickly and are often deliberately cautious. Low precision may mean the reference missed flooding, not that we invented it.

Coverage matters. The reference files contain only flood polygons, not the area each product actually checked, so everything outside the polygons is treated as dry. That assumption affects precision, F1, overall accuracy and κ. Recall does not depend on it.

Our map has precision 0.33 and recall 0.78 against MBRSC. In plain words?

We found 78% of the flooding that MBRSC mapped, but only a third of what we mapped is also in the MBRSC map. Our map is much larger (303 km² against 128 km²), and MBRSC's map is likely conservative.

The pipeline, step by step

The work is split between two places. Earth Engine steps run on Google's servers, which hold the satellite archive and do the heavy per-image processing. local steps run on your computer in Python, which made it fast to rerun the classification many times for the sensitivity tests.

  1. Get the boundary and reference maps

    Download district and sub-district boundaries and the reference flood maps, build the June 2022 Nagaon boundary (Hojai excluded), and clip the reference maps to it.

    localsrc/download_data.py, src/prepare_boundary.py, src/clip_reference.py

  2. List the available radar images

    Find every Sentinel-1 image over the district from January to July 2022, with its pass, track and how much of the district it covers. Three tracks exist; track 77 (100% coverage) and track 41 (93%) are used, track 143 (55%) is not.

    Earth Enginenotebooks/01_inventory.ipynb → outputs/tables/s1_inventory*.csv

  3. Clean each image and build dry-season baselines

    Mask noisy image borders (VV below −30 dB), apply the Lee speckle filter, and take the median of the January to mid-April images of each track as its dry baseline.

    Earth Enginesrc/floodmap.py (preprocess, lee_filter, baseline, event_image)

  4. Download analysis-ready stacks

    For each June date, download one file holding the June VV and VH and the baseline VV and VH. Also download a "static" file with HAND, slope, JRC water and WorldCover. All are placed on the same 10 m UTM 46N grid. Values are stored as whole numbers (dB × 100) to save space. This took about an hour on the free Earth Engine tier.

    Earth Enginelocalsrc/fetch_stacks.py, src/download.py → outputs/rasters/stacks/

  5. Classify flooding on each date

    Compute the edge-based Otsu threshold, apply the two-condition rule, remove high, steep and permanent-water pixels and small patches, and save a flood map per date with classes 0 dry, 1 flood, 2 permanent water, 255 not observed.

    localsrc/classify.py, notebooks/02_flood_detection.ipynb → outputs/rasters/flood_*.tif

  6. Measure areas

    Count flood pixels per date, per sub-district, and within the area seen on all six dates (the common footprint, needed because track 41 misses the district's eastern tip).

    localnotebooks/02_flood_detection.ipynb → flood_area_by_date.csv, flood_area_by_subdistrict.csv

  7. Measure settlement exposure

    Compute distance to the nearest flood pixel and count WorldCover built-up land within 30, 50 and 100 m, per date and for all dates combined. Also count flood frequency (how many dates each pixel was flooded) and summarise exposure on a 1 km grid.

    localnotebooks/03_exposure.ipynb → exposure_by_*.csv, flood_frequency_june2022.tif, exposure_grid_1km.gpkg

  8. Test sensitivity to settings

    Rerun the classification with each setting varied (thresholds, HAND, slope, water mask, patch size, polarisation) on 16, 18 and 30 June, plus a joint grid of the two main thresholds on 18 June.

    localnotebooks/04_sensitivity.ipynb → sensitivity_*.csv, sensitivity_*.png

  9. Compare with reference maps and published figures

    Compute precision, recall, F1, κ and more against the MBRSC and ALOS-2 maps, check agreement at 100 m, 500 m and 1 km, break disagreements down by land cover, compare sub-district rankings, and compare total areas with the NRSC figures and Irukumati et al. (2025).

    localnotebooks/05_validation.ipynb → validation_*.csv

  10. Make maps and write up

    Draw the report maps, write QGIS styles so the rasters display correctly in QGIS, and compile the LaTeX report.

    localnotebooks/06_maps.ipynb, src/write_qgis_styles.py, docs/report/report.tex

What the project found

Research question 1: change through June

Flooding was limited in early June (about 5% of the district), rose sharply by 18 June, and was largest at the end of the month. In total 766 km² flooded on at least one date and 405 km² on three or more. Kampur was worst hit on 18 June (171 km²); Raha and Nagaon sub-districts were worst on 28–30 June.

Research question 2: built-up land near flooding

7.2 km² of built-up land (11% of the district's 63.9 km²) was within 50 m of flood water on some June date. Nagaon sub-district had the largest area (1.64 km², it contains the town); Raha (25%) and Kampur (23%) had the highest share of their built-up land exposed. Much of the exposed "built-up" land is roads and the houses along them, running between flooded fields.

Research question 3: sensitivity

  • The drop threshold matters most (on 18 June, 358–574 km² over −1 to −6 dB), followed by the absolute VH threshold (373–534 km²).
  • Over common choices of both, 18 June ranges 423–575 km², about ±15% around the default.
  • The 2021 water mask changes results by 16–19%; HAND and slope by less than 1% because the district is flat.
  • Exposure is more sensitive than area, especially to the distance used (2.6 km² at 30 m against 22.3 km² at 100 m).

Agreement with other maps

  • 30 June: 600.1 km² against NRSC's 597.32 km², a 0.5% difference. 18 June: 503 km² against NRSC's 413 km², about 22% higher.
  • Against MBRSC (same 16 June image): recall 0.78, precision 0.33, F1 0.46, κ 0.42.
  • Against ALOS-2 (22 June, L-band): recall about 0.50. Half the flooding only ALOS-2 found is under trees, consistent with C-band missing water beneath canopy, though the scene dates and methods also differ.
  • Extra flooding in our maps is mostly cropland, often land that has held water before: likely waterlogged paddy and seasonal wetlands.

Glossary

Ascending / descending pass
Satellite moving south-to-north (evening over India) or north-to-south (early morning).
Backscatter
Radar energy returned to the satellite from the ground.
Baseline
Reference image of normal conditions; here the dry-season median of the same track.
Bimodal
A histogram with two peaks, such as water and land.
C-band / L-band
Radar wavelengths of about 5.6 cm (Sentinel-1) and about 24 cm (ALOS-2). Longer wavelengths penetrate vegetation better.
Canny edge detector
An algorithm that finds sharp boundaries in an image.
Change detection
Finding what changed between a reference image and an event image.
Common footprint
The part of the district observed on all six dates (2,389.9 km²).
Confusion matrix
Table of TP, FP, FN and TN counts from comparing two maps.
Connected components
Groups of touching pixels treated as one patch.
CRS / EPSG code
Coordinate reference system and its numeric ID, e.g. EPSG:32646 for UTM 46N.
dB (decibel)
Logarithmic scale: 10 × log10 of the linear value. −3 dB is about half.
DEM
Digital elevation model; a raster of ground height.
Distance transform
Gives each pixel its distance to the nearest pixel of a target, here flood.
Double bounce
Radar reflecting off the ground then a wall straight back; makes buildings bright.
Earth Engine (GEE)
Google's cloud platform holding satellite archives and running analysis on them.
Edge-based Otsu
Otsu thresholding applied only near land/water edges so both classes are represented.
Exposure
Built-up land close to mapped flood water; an indicator of what was at risk, not damage.
F1 score
Harmonic mean of precision and recall.
GeoJSON / GeoPackage / shapefile
Common vector file formats.
GeoTIFF
Image file format that stores where the raster sits on Earth.
Geodesic area
Area measured on the curved Earth rather than on a flat map.
GRD
Ground Range Detected: a standard Sentinel-1 product with brightness values on a ground grid.
HAND
Height above nearest drainage: how far a pixel sits above the nearest stream.
Incidence angle
The angle at which the radar beam meets the ground; differs between tracks.
IoU
Intersection over union: overlap divided by combined area.
JRC Global Surface Water
Landsat-based record of water, 1984–2021. Occurrence covers all years; seasonality covers 2021.
Kappa (κ)
Agreement adjusted for agreement expected by chance.
Lee filter
Speckle filter that smooths flat areas while keeping edges.
Median composite
For each pixel, the middle value of several images; resistant to one odd image.
Minimum mapping unit (MMU)
Smallest patch kept; here 8 pixels (800 m²).
Nodata
A value meaning "no information"; 255 in the flood maps.
Otsu's method
Automatic threshold that best separates two groups in a histogram.
Overall accuracy
Share of pixels both maps label the same; inflated when one class dominates.
Pixel
One cell of a raster; 10 m × 10 m here.
Polarisation (VV, VH)
Orientation of sent and received radar waves. VH is cross-polarised and used for detection here.
Precision / recall
Share of our flood the reference agrees with / share of the reference flood we found.
Raster / vector
Grid of pixel values / points, lines and polygons.
Rasterise
Convert polygons to pixels on a grid.
Relative orbit (track)
Number identifying a repeating orbit path; same track means same viewing geometry.
Revisit time
How often a place is imaged; 12 days per track in 2022.
SAR
Synthetic aperture radar: side-looking imaging radar that works through cloud and at night.
Sensitivity analysis
Testing how results change when settings change.
Sigma nought (σ⁰)
Backscatter per unit ground area; the value in each radar pixel.
Specular reflection
Mirror-like reflection away from the sensor; why calm water looks dark.
Speckle
Grainy noise in radar images from random interference within each pixel.
Terrain correction
Placing each radar pixel at its true map position using elevation data.
Threshold
Cut-off value separating classes.
UTC / IST
Universal time / Indian time (UTC + 5:30).
UTM
Universal Transverse Mercator; metric map projection in 6° zones. Zone 46N covers 90–96°E.
Volume scattering
Radar scattering inside a canopy; hides water under trees.
WorldCover
ESA 10 m land-cover map for 2021; class 50 is built-up.
Zonal statistics
Summaries of a raster within polygons, e.g. km² flooded per sub-district.

No terms match. Try a shorter word.
